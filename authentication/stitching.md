## Lead

**Vintage Coach is not saddle-stitched.** NYC-era and later US-made
bags use **machine lock-stitch** on virtually every seam, hand-finished
only on specific accents like turnlock backplates and strap junctions
[coachbagrehab-dating, vintagecoachie, thriftyrunner-authenticating].
The "saddle-stitched" line is the single most common piece of folklore
in this category and gets repeated by reputable resale dealers. It's
wrong.

The reliable diagnostic is not stitch type but **the combination of
consistent stitches per inch (SPI), heavy bonded thread, and clean
lock-off endings**. The 1985 catalog publishes the brand spec
explicitly: **12 stitches per inch** [coach-catalogs-1960-1989] —
the strongest primary SPI anchor in the archive. A bag claiming 1980s
manufacture should hit ~12 SPI; meaningful deviation is a flag.

## Saddle-stitch vs lock-stitch myth

The community frequently describes vintage Coach as "saddle-stitched." This
is wrong as a blanket claim. Per research/E §7 and the Tier 3 sources that
underlie it, **NYC-era bags use machine lock-stitch for most seams (despite
folklore), with hand-finishing on specific accents** [coachbagrehab-dating,
vintagecoachie, thriftyrunner-authenticating].

True saddle-stitch is a hand technique using two needles and a single
thread, producing characteristic two-strand interlock that does not unravel
when one stitch breaks. Coach NYC-era output volume alone makes universal
saddle-stitching implausible; inspection of construction confirms machine
lock-stitch as the norm.

Hand-finishing is documented on specific accents — turnlock backplates,
strap junctions, and select binding seams on smaller leather goods — but
the body and gusset seams of standard bags (Court, Willis, Bonnie, Bedford,
etc.) are machine-sewn. Per-model pages should record which accents on
which models show hand-finishing as primary inspections accumulate.

Confidence: tentative. The correction itself is well-supported across Tier
3 community guides, but the precise list of hand-finished accents per
model is not yet anchored to a primary source.

## Stitches per inch (SPI)

SPI is the single most useful per-bag diagnostic. Authentic NYC-era and
1980s US-made bags show **consistent SPI across a given seam**, with the
SPI itself falling within an era-typical range that should be recorded
on each per-model page as inspections accumulate.

**Primary anchor: 12 stitches per inch** documented in 1985 Coach
catalogs [coach-catalogs-1960-1989]. This is the strongest primary
SPI anchor in the archive — a 1985 brand-published spec for the Cahn
era, three months after Sara Lee acquisition. A vintage Coach claiming
1980s manufacture should hit ~12 SPI; meaningful deviation is a flag.

Diagnostic principles:

- **Consistency within a seam** matters more than the absolute SPI value.
  Variation in SPI within the same seam — bunched stitches in one section,
  stretched in another — is a fake or poor-reproduction tell.
- **Symmetry across mirrored seams** (left and right gusset, paired strap
  attachments) is expected on authentic bags.
- **Era ranges** vary; NYC-era machine output and later US-plant output
  use different industrial machines. Per-era SPI ranges are an open
  research item — see below.

Confidence: tentative. The principle is standard leather-goods QC; the
specific era ranges are not yet measured.

## Hand-folded seam binding (1989+ brand-voice anchor)

The hand-folded seam binding is one of Coach's most cited
brand-published construction diagnostics. **Primary documentation
predates 1991 by at least 2 years**: the Summer 1989 catalog (pages
54-55) describes the construction in detail [coach-catalogs-1960-1989]:

> "The seam binding of every Coach bag and briefcase is a detail
> that contributes to the distinctive Coach look as well as to the
> durability of our products. We use a special process to make this
> binding from the very same glove-tanned cowhide as the bag or
> briefcase itself. The seam binding is folded around every edge and
> sewn through several layers of leather. Creating the close, even
> stitches found on our fine leather-goods requires great skill and
> many years of experience."

The 1985 catalog (research stream M1) also documents **outside-seam
construction with continuous leather binding** as the Coach portfolio
signature — the same construction principle, framed for portfolios
specifically. So the hand-folded seam binding diagnostic is in
brand-published copy by 1985 and in full-detail brand-voice form by
Summer 1989, not first introduced in 1991 as earlier archive framing
implied.

Authentication implications:
- The seam binding is **glove-tanned cowhide cut from the same leather
  as the bag** — a Coach NYC-era bag with binding in a different
  material (vinyl, contrast leather not from the same hide) is wrong.
- The binding is **folded around the edge and sewn through multiple
  layers** — a Coach bag with a flat or unfolded seam binding is
  inconsistent with brand-published construction.

## Thread

Authentic vintage Coach uses **heavy bonded nylon or polyester thread**,
color-matched to the body leather [vintagecoachbags-authenticity,
thriftyrunner-authenticating]. The thread is substantial — visibly thicker
than dress-shirt or fashion-bag thread — and lies flat in the stitch
channel.

- **Color match** is the default. British tan body → tan thread; black
  body → black thread.
- **Contrasting thread** is model-specific, not general. Sheridan and some
  Madison variants use contrast stitching as a designed accent. Contrast
  thread on a model that historically used matched thread is a fake tell.
- **Thin or shiny thread** (consumer-grade polyester) on a bag claiming
  pre-1995 manufacture is a fake or reproduction signal.

Confidence: tentative. Thread material specifications are not documented
in any primary source yet located.

## Lock-stitch endings

Authentic machine lock-stitch terminates with a **clean backstitch** —
two or three stitches reversed over the seam end, then trimmed flush.
On the inside of the bag (and on the underside of straps), authentic
lock-off endings are nearly invisible.

Tells of poor reproduction:

- **Loose tail threads** hanging from seam ends.
- **Visible knots** on the inside or underside of seams. Lock-stitch
  does not require knots; visible knots indicate either hand-tied
  finishing on a machine seam (a quality red flag) or a different
  stitch technique entirely.
- **Frayed thread ends** without backstitch reinforcement; these
  unravel with use, which authentic Coach construction prevents.

Confidence: tentative. The principle is standard industrial sewing
practice; per-model documentation of where lock-offs are visible
versus hidden is an open item.

## Open verification items

1. **Measured SPI ranges per era** from inspection of bags with
   independent date provenance (dated catalog appearance, original
   receipt, or Tier 1 documentation). Until measured ranges exist,
   "SPI consistency" is a relative diagnostic only.
2. **Primary source for the saddle-stitch correction.** The correction
   appears across Tier 3 community guides but has not been anchored to
   a Coach catalog, press piece, or factory documentation. A 1970s or
   1980s catalog that describes construction in Coach's own words
   would settle the question.
3. **Per-model hand-finished accents inventory.** Which specific
   accents on which models show hand-finishing — to be built up on
   per-model pages as inspections accumulate.
4. **Thread material confirmation.** Bonded nylon vs polyester is
   community consensus; a primary source (factory spec sheet from the
   Cashin papers `ucla-cashin-papers`, or a Coach S-1 / 10-K
   manufacturing description) would anchor the claim.
